# When masking runs out: midlife stress and late diagnosis in women

{% include post-meta.html post=page %}

> *A note before you start.* This piece isn't about pitting women against men. It's a look at a gap in the research on adult neurodivergence, and where I quote figures comparing women and men, they're the figures the studies report. As a neurodivergent man myself, I don't believe autism and ADHD are really more common in one biological sex than the other, and there's growing evidence for that view. [^1][^2][^3][^4][^5] What differs, I think, is when and how we're noticed.

Since my books came out, I've talked with a lot of readers and people from my audiences. A surprising number of them are women in their 40s, 50s and early 60s who've only just been diagnosed autistic, ADHD, or both.

Most of them tell me the same story. They coped, and masked, for decades. Then something changed in midlife, very often perimenopause, and the coping stopped working. Many had autistic shutdowns or burnout. And only then did anyone, including them, start to wonder whether they were autistic or ADHD.

I'm well aware that's anecdotal. But a small number of published accounts say much the same thing, [^6] and it leaves me with a question I keep coming back to. If masking was already using up most of what these women had, did the extra stress of midlife, with perimenopause on top, push them past the point where they could keep it up? And if so, is that part of why so many women are diagnosed so late?

## <i class="fas text-body-tertiary">1</i> Masking was never free

Everyone masks to some degree; it's part of fitting into any group. The autistic version is harder work. It means forcing eye contact, rehearsing conversations, copying the people around us and holding back whatever might give us away, and it never becomes the easy habit it does for most people. I've written about the difference in [*Everyone Masks*](/essays/everyone-masks.html). The costs described by Hull and colleagues included exhaustion, and damage to how people saw themselves. [^7]

More specifically, Leedham and colleagues interviewed 11 women who were diagnosed autistic between the ages of 40 and 62. Some said the effort it took to fit in had left them very unhappy and worn out. [^8]

Women with ADHD mask too, although it's been studied far less. Kelly and colleagues interviewed 12 women aged 45 to 58 who were diagnosed with ADHD as adults. They described hiding traits like talking too much or seeming disorganised so they'd come across as acceptable, and feeling pressure to keep their homes and lives perfectly in order. The researchers noted that looking as if you're coping can hide a lot of effort and distress. Several of the women also said perimenopause and menopause seemed to make their emotions harder to manage. [^9]

When masking finally fails, what follows has a name. Raymaker and colleagues, working with autistic adults, describe autistic burnout as long-lasting exhaustion, loss of skills and lower tolerance for sensory input. They trace it to years of life stress, and to expectations that outstrip what a person can manage without support. [^10] Higgins and colleagues, also working with autistic adults, put the fatigue of masking first in their list of what comes before it. [^11]

My assumption is that masking is a stressor that never switches off. McEwen called the wear and tear of long-term stress *allostatic load*: the same body systems that protect us in a crisis start to do damage when they're kept running for years. [^12] A woman who has masked since primary school has been carrying that load for thirty or forty years. She can look completely fine from the outside and still have very little in reserve.

## <i class="fas text-body-tertiary">2</i> The load men don't carry

Perimenopause is the transition from the reproductive years to menopause, as the ovaries gradually wind down. It usually takes several years, and most often starts in a woman’s 40s. Oestrogen and progesterone don’t simply fall during this time; they swing up and down, often without any pattern. The rate of depression rises two to threefold during the transition. [^13]

Gordon and colleagues reviewed the evidence and proposed a reason. Progesterone is the raw material for a calming chemical in the brain, and as the hormones swing, that calming effect may become less reliable. In their model, this makes the body's stress system more reactive. And the hormone changes don't act alone; they interact with whatever stress a woman's life already holds at midlife.

That's the part that caught my attention. If masking is a long-running stressor, then the women carrying the most of it are the ones Gordon's model would expect to be hit hardest.

The ADHD evidence is newer. In an Icelandic study of 5,392 women aged 35 to 55, Jakobsdóttir Smári and colleagues found severe perimenopausal symptoms in 54 per cent of women with ADHD, against 30 per cent of women without it. [^14] The gap was biggest at ages 35 to 39. Women with ADHD had their worst symptoms about ten years earlier than the others, and the authors suggest perimenopause may start earlier in ADHD. The ADHD diagnoses were self-reported, though, and the study is a single snapshot, so it can't show what caused what.

Before that, Dorani and colleagues surveyed 209 Dutch women with ADHD. [^15] They found high rates of mood symptoms tied to hormone changes: before periods, after a first childbirth, and around menopause. They compared those rates with figures from the general population, not with a matched group of women without ADHD.

## <i class="fas text-body-tertiary">3</i> "When my autism broke"

Research on menopause in autistic women is still in its infancy, and very sparse. Moseley and colleagues ran an online focus group with seven autistic people aged 49 to 63. [^6] They said menopause made almost everything harder: social communication, sensory sensitivity, planning, emotions, sleep, and looking after themselves. Masking became much harder to keep up, and coping skills that had worked for years stopped working. One woman reported around three meltdowns a week during that time. She also gave the paper its title, describing it as the time her autism "broke".

The same team then interviewed 17 autistic people about menopause. [^16] They concluded that menopause could seriously harm the health and wellbeing of autistic people, and that their support needs weren't being met. One of their main themes was the long road many of them had taken to recognise their autism as adults.

At first glance, the link to diagnosis looks thinner than the stories I hear. In Moseley's focus group, only one person was diagnosed at the point of menopause. But that may be the wrong thing to look for. If perimenopause is part of what breaks the coping, the diagnosis would come afterwards, once someone has worked out what's going on and found a way to be assessed, and that can take years. Two of the participants described that order: the menopausal changes came first, and they linked finding out about their autism to how hard that time had been. [^6]

In Leedham's 11 women diagnosed after 40, menopause doesn't come up at all. [^8] Menopause wasn't something that study set out to look at, so its absence doesn't tell us much either way. [^a] These accounts fit what I hear at talks, but they come from small groups, and none of them were set up to check timing.

## <i class="fas text-body-tertiary">4</i> It's never just one thing

Perimenopause doesn't arrive on its own. Midlife tends to bring teenagers, ageing parents and the busiest years of a career all at about the same time. For a lot of these women, it also brings a child being assessed for autism or ADHD.

That last one counts twice. In an Australian oral history study of 26 adults diagnosed autistic after 35, many said their own diagnosis followed their child's. [^17] A child's assessment adds to the load, and it can also be the moment a mother recognises herself.

So I don't think perimenopause works alone. It's one more load on top of the others, and it's the one men never carry. [^b] That's close to what Gordon's model says: the hormone swings work together with whatever stress is already there. [^13] From the outside, all anyone sees is the shutdown or the burnout. They don't see the pile of things underneath it.

There's a strong alternative, though. Adults, and women in particular, are being recognised far more than they used to be. In UK GP records, new autism diagnoses rose by 787 per cent between 1998 and 2018, with the biggest rises among adults and among females. [^18] The authors put most of that down to better recognition, not more autism. A woman who is 50 now grew up when hardly anyone thought girls could be autistic. Some of the midlife peak may simply be the first time anyone was looking.

Comparing women with men helps more than I expected. In a 2025 Scottish study of 408 people diagnosed by 30 clinical teams, boys outnumbered girls about two to one among autistic children under 10, but among autistic adults the numbers were roughly even. [^2] In Taiwan's national health records from 2000 to 2007, more adult women than men were newly diagnosed with ADHD. [^19] I think autism and ADHD are close to even between men and women, and that the old ratios mostly reflect who gets noticed in childhood. If that's right, the missing women have to turn up somewhere later in life. Autism and ADHD are lifelong, so nothing in midlife can cause either. My point is that the pile-up of midlife stress may make the traits harder to hide, which would make midlife one of the places the missing women finally show up. [^c]

That comparison can't settle much on its own, though. Men carry most of the same midlife loads, apart from perimenopause, and many are still living under masculine norms. Wong and colleagues pooled 78 samples and found that men who stick closely to norms like self-reliance and keeping emotions under control are less willing to seek psychological help. [^20] A man of 50 who is struggling may be no more willing to say so than he was at 15. So if more women than men are diagnosed at 40 to 55, it could be perimenopause adding weight, men not coming forward, or both, and the numbers alone can't tell us which. I think the answer has to come from asking people what was going on in their lives when it all fell apart.

And the ADHD numbers don't fit as neatly as I'd like either. In the Icelandic study, severe current ADHD symptoms were most common at ages 35 to 39, and less common in each older age group. [^14] If the extra load of perimenopause were pushing women with ADHD past what they could cope with, I'd have expected that figure to climb into the late 40s. It could still fit, if perimenopause really does start earlier in ADHD, as the authors suggest. But that's a lot to hang on one survey.

There's a risk on the other side as well. When the crash does come, it doesn't arrive with a label. In a Dutch register of 1,211 autistic adults, about a quarter said they'd been given a psychiatric diagnosis before autism that they believe was wrong, and women said so nearly twice as often as men. Burnout-related disorders were among the diagnoses they named. [^21] A woman who falls apart at 48 can easily be told it's depression, or burnout, or "just menopause".

What nobody seems to have done yet is put these pieces side by side. I couldn't find anyone who has asked women diagnosed after 40 what stage of menopause they were at, and what else was going on in their lives, when it happened. Nor could I find anyone who has followed women through perimenopause closely enough to see masking, stress and hormones change from one day to the next. [^d] I'd love to see someone ask women several times a day, over a couple of weeks, how stressed they are, how much effort fitting in is taking and how much they've got left, and line that up against their stress hormones and ovarian hormones, measured from saliva, and against what else is going on for them.

## What I think

My guess is that it's rarely one thing that tips the balance. A woman who has masked for forty years is running with very little margin. Midlife then piles on more: teenagers, ageing parents, a demanding job, maybe a child's diagnosis, and for women, perimenopause on top, which can make the stress system itself more reactive. Any one of those might be manageable. All of them together can use up what's left, and what people see is the shutdown. If that drop in capacity could be spotted from day to day, help could arrive before the shutdown, not after it.

I could be wrong. It might turn out to be mostly better recognition, with midlife simply being when it caught up with these women.

But it changes what I'd want a neurodivergent woman in her 40s to hear when she's suddenly not coping. "It's just hormones" or "it's just stress" sends her home with nothing. If midlife is exposing something that was always there, the more useful question is what she's been carrying all these years, and whether she's autistic, ADHD, or both.
