# Why I don't use social media

{% include post-meta.html post=page %}

People ask why I don't use social media. Privacy is good part of the answer, but that's not all.

There is the time it takes, and the superficial and meaningless comparison with other people - but the thing I keep coming back to is friendship. Friendship is not a metric - neither in count, or in strength - and some arbitrary company has no right to who, what, or how I interact with my true relationships, nor should they collect information about who I talk to or what we share.

I have listed eight reasons below as to why I stay away. Some have good research behind them. Others are about what I want from my own life. They don't all carry the same weight - and on some others may disagree - but they're strong enough for me to have a visceral distaste for social media.

## <i class="fas text-body-tertiary">1</i> The perpetual comparison is harmful

Psychologists studied comparison long before social media existed. When we compare ourselves with someone we see as better off, they call it "upward comparison." Gerber, Wheeler and Suls reviewed more than sixty years of research and found that people generally preferred this kind of comparison. It often left them feeling worse and judging their own abilities more harshly. [^40]

McCarthy and Morina found a related pattern in research on depression and anxiety. Among people with mental health conditions, judging themselves unfavourably against others was associated with worse symptoms. That does not prove that comparison caused those symptoms, but it gives me reason to take the habit seriously. [^41]

Comparison can also inspire us. My objection is to making it a constant part of my day.

And social media is specifically designed for this! You do not even have to post anything. Scrolling through other people's lives without talking to anyone is what researchers call “passive use.” Verduyn and colleagues found that this kind of Facebook use lowered wellbeing, with envy helping to explain the effect. [^4]

There is broader evidence too. When Facebook first opened, it reached US universities at different times. Braghieri, Levy and Makarin used that rollout to study changes in student mental health across 775 colleges. Mental health worsened after Facebook arrived, especially among students in circumstances that could make social comparison harder. [^1][^a]

A later experiment paid 1,661 people to deactivate Facebook for four weeks before the 2018 US midterm elections. They gained about an hour a day and reported a small improvement in wellbeing. Political polarisation fell, but so did knowledge of the news. After the experiment, they used Facebook less and valued it less than before. [^2]

Hunt and colleagues found reductions in loneliness and depression when 143 undergraduates limited their use to ten minutes per platform each day for three weeks. [^3]

These findings don't settle the question for everyone. Orben and Przybylski found a tiny association between screen use and adolescent wellbeing. [^5] A review of twenty-five reviews found that most described the association as weak or inconsistent. [^6] Odgers argues, in a Nature book review, that claims about social media causing the teenage mental-health crisis run ahead of the evidence. [^7] That is her interpretation of the research. Publication in Nature does not make it the last word.

I don't need social media to be bad for everyone before I decide it isn't good for me. My concern is the comparison and the watching. Applying those findings to myself is my judgement, not a result from a study of autistic or ADHD adults. [^b]

Then there is the infamous fear of missing out, or FOMO. When your friends, news and invitations are all there, leaving costs you the convenience of keeping up without reaching out. But the effort matters to me. Making time for someone, and knowing they made time for me, is part of what gives a friendship value.

In the earlier Facebook experiment, people knew less about the news after a month away. They also valued Facebook less. Nine weeks later, one in twenty still had their accounts deactivated, without being paid to stay off. [^2] In Hunt's study, fear of missing out fell in both groups, even among people who weren't asked to cut back. The authors suggested that monitoring their own use helped. [^3]

I can accept missing some updates. I would rather spend that time keeping in touch with the people I care about - quality, not quantity.

## <i class="fas text-body-tertiary">2</i> The risks are not worth it

Australia's social media age restrictions took effect on 10 December 2025. Platforms covered by the law must take reasonable steps to prevent under-sixteens from holding accounts. The penalties fall on platforms, not children or parents. [^8][^9]

Similarly, the UK's child protection rules took a different approach. Ofcom's codes took effect on 25 July 2025, with requirements for age assurance and for filtering specified harmful material from children's feeds. [^10]

This is two governments imposing enforceable rules on platforms to protect children. Compliance is not optional.  Children are still developing the judgement needed to assess risks and consequences. And the companies cannot be trusted to volunteer what they know: in 2021, Frances Haugen testified that Facebook withheld information and misled the public about what its own research revealed about children’s safety. [^42]

As an adult, I get to make that judgement for myself. Being allowed to accept a risk does not make it disappear. [^d] Nor does avoiding social media mean abandoning the internet. Australia's rules distinguish social media from messaging, gaming, professional engagement, education and health support, which have exemptions. [^c] I can use those services without spending my time in a social media feed.

Most people would avoid a dark alley in a shady part of town, even knowing they would probably get through unharmed. The small chance of something going badly wrong is enough to make them take another route. Yet with social media, we often set that caution aside. That is easier to do when the company selling you the service keeps the risks out of sight.

For me, social media doesn't offer enough to justify the time and the risks. I can make that decision without waiting for a ban, or asking anyone else to make it with me.

## <i class="fas text-body-tertiary">3</i> I don't trust platforms with my data

I used to say I didn't post much (or at all). I find it less reassuring now, because posting is only a small part of what an account allows a platform to do.

For example, round 53 Australians installed an app called *This Is Your Digital Life*. Yet the Australian Information Commissioner's case concerned information about more than 311,000 Australians. Almost all of them hadn't installed the app. Their Facebook friends had, and that was enough to expose their information. Meta settled the proceedings for $50 million in December 2024. [^11][^12]

Another case concerned Facebook receiving browsing histories from other websites after users logged out. The US Court of Appeals reinstated the claims in 2020, and Facebook settled for US$90 million in 2022. [^13]

In 2025, a California jury found Meta liable for receiving reproductive health information sent by a period-tracking app. The information included cycle dates, sexual activity and pregnancy intentions. The class covered roughly 38 million people. These cases involved different laws, and settlements are not admissions of liability. [^14][^e]

Then theres LinkedIn's connection experiments - and they bother me for a different reason. Between 2015 and 2019, the company changed the mix of connections it recommended to about twenty million members, then measured the effect on employment. Members weren't told that they were in an experiment. LinkedIn said the work was consistent with its user agreement. [^15][^16]

The experiments produced useful research. They weren't found unlawful. [^f] But history is full of experiments that pursued useful knowledge while harming the people involved. Useful results do not excuse how researchers obtain them. Modern research ethics requires attention to the risks people bear and their freedom to choose whether to participate. [^43]

Having an account does not mean agreeing to become an experimental subject. And yes, there is the predictable answer: “You accepted the terms.” Even where those terms mention experiments, burying permission in a document people barely read does not establish informed consent. In a study of 543 people joining a fictional social network, 98% missed outrageous clauses, including one requiring their first-born child as payment. [^44] A company can point to a clause without answering whether anyone understood what they were agreeing to. I signed up to find professional contacts. If you want to experiment with something that can affect my job prospects, explain what you are doing and give me a choice. Pointing me back to the small print does not excuse keeping me uninformed.

In these examples not posting wouldn't have kept those members out of the experiments. Nor would it have protected the "friends" of the Australians who installed that app.

For me, I know I can't control every way a company collects information about me. But, I can decide whether to keep an account with it. That is a more useful choice than deciding how often to post.

## <i class="fas text-body-tertiary">4</i> Social media isn't about people anymore

In April 2025, Imperva reported that automated programs had overtaken people as the source of most web traffic in its measurements. Bots accounted for 51% of traffic during 2024, exceeding human activity for the first time in a decade. [^45] Its next report estimated that the share reached 53% in 2025. [^17][^g]

There are bots designed to pass as people, though. Ferrara and colleagues described their methods and influence a decade ago. [^18]

Social media is supposed to connect people. If I am talking to software pretending to be a person, that defeats the purpose. Being shown an advert is not connection either. It is marketing. We recognise that distinction on television, where a commercial break interrupts what we came to watch. Ad-free streaming offers a way to avoid those interruptions. [^46] Putting an advert between friends' posts does not turn it into human contact - nor does it offer a way to exclude being marketed to.

Imagine speaking in a dark room without knowing whether anyone is listening. Something answers, but you cannot tell whether it is another person, a recording or a sales pitch. How long would you keep talking? Yet on social media, those who post, post into that uncertainty and assume they have reached someone.

Me, I can choose which conversations to join and which to walk away from - I decide which ones I want no part of. A social media feed gives the platform a say in what reaches me. I can block an account after it shows me something ugly, but I have already seen it. I wanted contact with people I chose, yet I get whatever else the platform decides to put in front of me.

## <i class="fas text-body-tertiary">5</i> Finding something worth reading takes too much work

I am always looking for something new to learn. But on social media, more and more of that effort goes into separating useful knowledge from unsupported opinions and manufactured misinformation. I want to spend my time learning, not checking whether someone has simply made something up.

Social media companies are actively encouraging more generated content. In September 2025, Meta introduced Vibes, a feed of AI-generated videos within its Meta AI app. It lets users create and remix videos, then share them to Instagram and Facebook Stories and Reels. [^47] The company is making it easier to produce more material for its platforms. I still have to do the work of finding something worth my time. And more slop makes that harder.

AI assistance doesn't tell me whether a piece has anything to say. A person can use it to edit an account of something they actually did, fix grammar, paraphrase, etc. But generating another article from material already online doesn't, by itself, give us another person's experience - it just adds to the noise.

Shumailov and colleagues found that models trained repeatedly on generated data can lose the rare and unusual parts of the original data. [^20] That is a finding about training models, not proof that a social media feed is doing the same thing. It does help explain my concern about relying on recycled material.

The more immediate problem is time. Herbert Simon was already writing about the shortage of attention caused by too much information in 1971. [^21] Then came the web, putting libraries' worth of information from around the world within reach. Social media added feeds that keep delivering more, without waiting for us to finish what is already there. Now AI lets people generate another batch in seconds. Each stage adds more material and speeds up its arrival. But there is only so much I can read, however cheap it becomes for others to produce more.

Qiu and colleagues modelled a network where people had limited attention and memory. As the volume of information increased, the system became worse at separating good material from bad. Under realistic conditions in their model, quality offered little advantage in determining what spread. [^22][^i]

A study of product-review searches on Google, Bing and DuckDuckGo found that pages earning commissions from sales appeared much more often in the results than their share of reviews on the web suggested. All three engines were vulnerable to link spam. [^23][^i] The study examined search results. The parallel I see with social media is that content can be very good at getting in front of me without having much to offer once it does.

I want to learn something, and I resent how much time goes into sorting through rubbish before I can. I'd rather spend that time reading a book or a worthwhile article than keep scrolling in the hope that something useful turns up.

## <i class="fas text-body-tertiary">6</i> Social media pushes people into opposing camps

The familiar explanation is that social media traps us in echo chambers. I don't think that explains the whole problem.

The proverbial “algorithm” is designed to keep us engaged. Facebook describes ranking posts by predicting what people will interact with, using their previous behaviour to inform those predictions. [^48] What I readily accept is useful to that process: show me more of what I already agree with, and I have an easy reason to keep reading.

But that selection also determines what I miss. Cinelli and colleagues found that users on Facebook and Twitter clustered with like-minded people, with information spreading mainly within those groups. [^24] Facebook's own researchers found that feed ranking further reduced exposure to opposing news, although users' choices contributed more to that reduction. [^49] An alternative view does not have to be banned to become harder to find. I cannot judge an issue properly if important parts of it never reach me.

The effects are not as simple as “more agreement means more polarisation.” Researchers reduced like-minded content by about a third for 23,377 Facebook users during the 2020 US election. They found no detectable change in eight measures, including polarisation and belief in false claims. [^25][^j]

Nor does disagreement automatically produce better thinking. Bail's experiment exposed participants to opposing political views through a Twitter bot. Republican participants subsequently reported more conservative opinions. [^26] That does not tell me they failed to think. Considering an argument does not oblige anyone to accept it. The question is whether the way disagreement reaches us encourages consideration or simply another reaction.

Agreement is not the only way to hold attention. A study published in 2025 found that Twitter's engagement-based ranking amplified emotionally charged posts and hostility towards political opponents compared with a chronological feed. Users did not prefer the political posts the algorithm selected. [^50] Törnberg's model offers another explanation for division: digital media can sort people into political camps through interaction with opponents, rather than isolation from them. [^27]

That is why I see “the algorithm” as hostile to critical thinking. I need to question familiar conclusions, consider other explanations and decide whether the evidence holds up. A feed that keeps rewarding agreement and provoking arguments encourages me to react before I do that work.

The algorithm is not about expanding thought. It is about locking attention, and it does that by curbing thought: narrowing what reaches me, reinforcing what I already accept and serving disagreement as something to fight. I want to examine an idea on its merits. I don't want my thinking reduced to choosing a camp and defending it.

## <i class="fas text-body-tertiary">7</i> LinkedIn doesn't get an exception

People usually assume I must at least use LinkedIn. I spent thirty years in senior roles making hiring decisions. That experience is part of why I don't.

Start with the jobs themselves. A professional-looking advert doesn't establish that a vacancy exists, or that the recruiter represents the company. The FTC warns about fake recruiters on LinkedIn who seek money and personal details that can be used for identity theft. [^51] Recruitment can also provide cover for gathering sensitive information. In June 2026, the Five Eyes intelligence agencies warned about Chinese intelligence officers using fake companies and recruitment on professional networking sites. [^52]

A fake vacancy could also reveal which employees at a competitor are looking to leave. That is a possible abuse, rather than something I can establish from an advert. The applicant reveals information before knowing whether anyone actually intends to hire them.

On the other side of the process, a profile tells me what someone wants an employer to see. Even an entirely truthful work history leaves important questions unanswered. What did you contribute? What went wrong? What did you do about it?

Six thousand connections do not mean six thousand satisfied colleagues, managers or clients. The count doesn't distinguish people who know your work from strangers accepting invitations for reach. It doesn't tell me whether invitations were personal or automated. I need to know what those people can actually say about your work.

Written recommendations have the same weaknesses as ordinary references. Candidates choose people willing to recommend them. Those people might be friends, or colleagues who like them but know little about their work. Aamodt's review describes references that are overwhelmingly favourable and often disagree with one another. [^32][^n] LinkedIn makes the praise public. I don't see why an audience, including the candidate, would encourage greater candour.

Nor does a busy feed establish expertise. Marketing spiel and regurgitated AI slop can keep a name in circulation without telling me whether its owner can do the job. If I am hiring, I need evidence of work, not evidence that someone keeps posting.

AI adds another problem. Applicants can generate tailored applications and send them at a scale that makes individual assessment harder. Greenhouse reports that applications per job more than doubled between 2022 and 2025, while vacancies took longer to fill. [^54][^p] More applications arrive, but that doesn't mean employers learn more about the people applying.

Automated filtering is supposed to manage the flood. But when screening depends on matching keywords and rigid requirements, relevant skills can disappear because someone describes them differently. Harvard's *Hidden Workers* report describes hiring processes that systematically exclude qualified people. [^55][^q] AI-written applications can also gain an advantage unrelated to ability. In a controlled experiment, AI models favoured résumés they generated themselves over otherwise equivalent human-written versions. [^56][^r] The wording changes. The candidate's ability doesn't.

That is where digital recruitment loses its value for me. More adverts, more applications and more polished claims are no improvement if employers find it harder to identify who can actually do the work. Relevant experience gets buried in the flood, while an application written to satisfy the filter gets through. I see a process becoming worse at its purpose: finding the right person for the job.

The same confusion between presentation and ability appears in the problem I discussed in my writing about neurodiversity and employment. [^57] An autistic candidate can have stronger skills and still lose out because their social manner feels less familiar. Whelpley and May found that non-autistic candidates received better ratings when evaluators watched mock interviews. When evaluators read the transcripts instead, the autistic candidates received better ratings. The answers were the same. The social presentation changed the judgement. [^53]

That is a serious failure in how we select people. Someone can be awkward in conversation and excellent at the work. Someone else can be charming and contribute very little. Communication matters where the job requires it, but judging the relevant skill takes more than deciding whether I like the person. Hiring for social acceptance can exclude the candidate who would add more value.

LinkedIn can introduce people to employers, and the earlier experiments found that connections helped people move between jobs. [^15][^o] But I think its reputation now exceeds its value. Being buried in a flood of profiles and applications does nothing for a capable candidate. It does nothing for an employer struggling to find that candidate either. The promise was useful professional connections. When making those connections requires sorting through this much noise, much of that value disappears. I don't use LinkedIn because I no longer see enough professional benefit, or validity, to justify the effort of being there.

## <i class="fas text-body-tertiary">8</i> A "friend" count doesn't tell me who I can rely on

Dunbar asked two UK samples about their Facebook friendships. Average friend counts were about 155 and 183. People considered roughly a quarter of those genuine friends. They could turn to about four in a crisis and fourteen for sympathy. Those smaller circles resembled the ones found offline. [^34]

Those numbers make me question what social media actually adds to friendship. Hundreds of names, but still only a handful of people to turn to when things go wrong. Keeping in touch has value. But adding someone to a list doesn't create the relationship that makes them willing to help. That takes time together. Hall's research describes the hours people spend together as they become closer friends. [^35] Clicking “Add friend” doesn't do that work.

Turkle writes about the trade between more contact and less connection. [^36] For the friendships I value, I don't see what a public feed adds. Knowing someone well, trusting them and being there when they need me takes attention and effort. The platform doesn't supply any of that.

What social media does make easy is an exchange: react to my post and I'll react to yours; share my work and I'll promote yours. Researchers distinguish relationships built around repayment from relationships where people respond to one another's needs. [^58] If a connection depends on what each person gets back, it can fall apart when that exchange changes. Stop providing attention, access or favours, and the reason for keeping you around can disappear. I want friendships that survive the times when one of us has nothing to offer.

Meanwhile, the feed competes for the attention those friendships need. Someone's news arrives mixed with adverts, arguments and irrelevant posts. Keeping up with that stream takes time without necessarily bringing me closer to anyone. I don't want to mistake watching people's updates for taking an interest in their lives. I would rather call a friend, listen properly and make time for them.

## What I willingly give up

Leaving social media costs me access to people and communities. For autistic adults, that can be a serious loss.

Van Driel and colleagues describe what autistic adults value in these spaces: text instead of faces, time to compose an answer, and contact with people like them who aren't available nearby. [^37] I recognise those needs. They are reasons social media could work for me too. But for me, the cons far outweigh the pros.

I can't wave that away by telling people to find a better place. A Discord chat or a forum only helps if the people you need are there. For some, the alternative to the platform is being alone.

What I want from those spaces is the conversation and the belonging. Research on autistic community connectedness finds value in particular friendships, shared understanding and shared political purpose. [^38] Those things can also exist in smaller groups, without a public feed. Smaller spaces have their own problems, and I am not claiming that they are automatically safe. [^l]

That is the compromise I prefer. Other people will make a different one, especially if leaving means losing their community.

I write here instead. The pieces are long, and fewer people are likely to find them than if I put them in a feed. But anyone can read them without an account. I don't have to maintain a profile or keep up with a stream of other people's updates to publish something.

I still want to write, and I still want people in my life. But I also don't want social media enough to accept what comes with it.
